Quantum contextuality

Which features of quantum theory are genuinely nonclassical? Interference, no-cloning, teleportation, remote steering and even entanglement all have explicit classical models, such as Spekkens' toy theory. Generalized noncontextuality (Spekkens, 2005) is a notion of nonclassicality that is rigorously defined, testable on a single system, and tied to practical advantages. My thesis, supervised by Dr. Beata Zjawin at ICTQT Gdańsk, is on contextuality; my current focus is how typical it is, meaning how often randomly chosen states and measurements produce nonclassical statistics.

Framework and typicality results

An ontological model is noncontextual if operationally equivalent procedures are represented identically in the model, a version of Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles. This is equivalent to simplex-embeddability of the associated generalized probabilistic theory (GPT) fragment, so for a prepare-and-measure scenario the question reduces to a linear program. Its output, the robustness of contextuality r, is zero exactly when a classical explanation exists.

I'm studying Typicality of Contextuality (Rossi, Zjawin, Baldijão, Schmid, Selby, Sainz). Its main findings, as I understand them:

  • A sharp threshold. With n ≤ d² random states (four for a qubit), the scenario is noncontextual with probability one, since linearly independent states cannot witness contextuality. Above this, typicality rises quickly.
  • Pure states and projective measurements. Typicality exceeds 99% at around n = 7 states and m = 8 measurements. It tends to 1 as either number grows, but stays strictly below 1 for any finite scenario.
  • Noise. Mixedness slows the rise but does not remove it. With 20 projective measurements, n = 9 states suffice at purity ≥ 0.7, and n = 7 at purity ≥ 0.9. With unsharp measurements the requirement can reach n = 25.
  • Versus Bell nonlocality. In the prepare-and-measure picture, contextuality reaches near-certain typicality faster. The comparison is not quantitative, because the steered states in a Bell scenario obey no-signalling constraints that random sampling ignores.
  • Typical contextuality is not typical advantage. In 3-to-1 parity-oblivious multiplexing, random projective measurements are contextual 98.6% of the time but give about an 8% advantage over the best noncontextual strategy, against 18.3% for the optimal one. Random POVMs are contextual 15.2% of the time, for a 0.3% advantage.

Can entanglement through gravity be witnessed in the CMB?

During inflation, the expanding background drives each Fourier mode of the curvature perturbation $\zeta$ into a two-mode squeezed state, in which modes k and −k are entangled in the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen sense. Could that entanglement be witnessed in CMB data, and would a positive result say anything about gravity rather than about the inflaton field?

This is an independent side project alongside my thesis. The question was suggested to me by Marios Christodoulou (IQOQI Vienna), whose work concerns testing the quantum nature of gravity with table-top experiments. I am at the literature stage, have no results of my own yet, and am working toward a conference poster.

The obstruction

As Christodoulou pointed out, CMB maps are effectively position-type, equal-time measurements, while a witness needs a non-commuting, momentum-like variable as well. Outside the horizon $\zeta$ is conserved and its conjugate momentum is suppressed by the same squeezing that makes $\zeta$ so robust an observable (Maldacena; Martin and Vennin). His suggestion that differences of measurements at different redshifts might supply the missing variable connects naturally to Leggett–Garg-type tests, which use one system measured at several times.

Questions I plan to work on

  • Whether unequal-time observables, such as redshift tomography or Leggett–Garg-type constructions, can supply a usable non-commuting variable for $\zeta$.
  • Whether Ireland and Vennin's Wigner-negativity result for the EFT Goldstone mode transfers to the curvature perturbation at finite wavenumber.
  • Whether the delocalised-source construction of Chen and Giacomini, which goes beyond the Newtonian potential in the laboratory, has a cosmological analogue. As far as I have found, this has not been attempted.
What I've been working through, and the reading list
  • Decoherence. The Polarski–Starobinsky picture of squeezing-induced classicality, Hsiang and Hu's argument that squeezing alone cannot produce true decoherence in a closed system, and the open-system calculations (e.g. Burgess et al., 2023) of decoherence from short-wavelength modes.
  • Non-Gaussianity. Gaussian squeezed states have non-negative Wigner functions, so any genuine non-classicality must come from non-Gaussian structure. I'm reading Inflation is Not Magic (Haque et al.) and Ireland and Vennin's work on negativity beyond slow roll.
  • Framing. The (k, −k) split is not a privileged bipartition, since a rotation to standing-wave modes makes the same state a product state. The squeezing is driven by a classical background, as in parametric down-conversion with a classical pump. Real-space entanglement is generic to any quantum field. Equal-time statistics of $\zeta$ alone always have a classical stand-in, because the observables commute.
  • The inference to gravity. Anastopoulos and Hu argue that, in the weak-field regime, such experiments probe only gravity's constraint sector, in analogy with the Coulomb term in QED. Whether entanglement implies a quantum mediator is also under active debate following Aziz and Howl (Nature, 2025).

Magnetic fields from ultralight dark matter

The origin of cosmic magnetic fields is unknown. Blazar observations point to a lower bound of roughly 10−17 G on scales of about 0.1 Mpc and above, inferred from the absence of expected GeV–TeV cascade emission; CMB observations give an upper bound of roughly 10−9 G; and inflationary and phase-transition mechanisms both struggle to deliver a large enough coherence length. This undergraduate project, supervised by Prof. Koushik Dutta at IISER Kolkata, asked whether an oscillating ultralight dark matter field can generate such fields after recombination. Brandenberger, Fröhlich and Jiao find of order 10−15 G at 1 Mpc; Sharma, Majumdar and Sachdeva then showed that the residual ionization left after recombination makes the gauge field heavily overdamped and suppresses the resonance. Two workarounds have since been proposed (see below).

  • Treating dark matter density fluctuations as noise on the driving term, which should enlarge the Floquet exponent and may soften the conductivity bound.
Mechanism, obstruction and reading list

Mechanism. Dark matter is an axion-like field ϕ coupled to photons through ϕ F F̃. Once the Hubble rate falls below the field's mass, ϕ oscillates, making one circular polarization of the gauge field tachyonically unstable during part of each period. The resonance produces a helical magnetic field with a spectrum peaked near kc ≃ g m ϕ0 a, after recombination, when Thomson scattering no longer screens the photon. The field at 1 Mpc depends steeply on the coupling and on a backreaction parameter F, the fraction of the dark matter energy that can drain into photons before the resonance shuts off; the original paper leaves F free. Follow-up papers extend the idea to a scalar (dilaton-like) coupling, which gives no helicity but a time-varying fine-structure constant, to a second narrow-resonance channel with a first backreaction estimate, to seeding supermassive black holes, and to a non-Abelian dark matter condensate acting through an indirect cascade.

Obstruction. Recombination is never complete. A residual free-electron fraction of about 10−4 gives the post-recombination universe a conductivity σ that exceeds the Hubble rate by roughly 26 orders of magnitude. The gauge-field equation is then heavily overdamped, and the growth rate is suppressed by a factor of order H/σ instead of growing exponentially; a growth rate above the Hubble rate would require a coupling several orders of magnitude above what observations allow. The authors confirm this numerically with the Pencil Code. Two proposed ways around it are to move the resonance to a dark photon and transfer the field by kinetic mixing (Alexander et al.), and to produce the field earlier, during radiation domination, using a delayed oscillation (Gerlach, Schmitt and Schwaller).
