Quantum Contextuality

MS Thesis Project · Supervised by Dr. Beata Zjawin (ICTQT, Poland) · [August 2026 - Present]

I'm an MS student in physics at IISER Kolkata. My thesis is on contextuality in quantum foundations, supervised by Dr. Beata Zjawin at the International Centre for Theory of Quantum Technologies (ICTQT) Gdańsk, Poland.

The question

Which features of quantum theory are genuinely nonclassical? Many of the usual candidates (interference, no-cloning, teleportation, remote steering, even entanglement) have explicit classical models, such as Spekkens' toy theory.

One notion remains that is rigorously defined, directly testable on a single system, and tied to practical advantages: generalized noncontextuality (Spekkens, 2005). An ontological model is noncontextual if operationally equivalent procedures are represented identically in the model, a version of Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles. This is equivalent to simplex-embeddability of the associated generalized probabilistic theory (GPT) fragment. Deciding it for a prepare-and-measure scenario therefore reduces to a linear program whose output, the robustness of contextuality r, is zero exactly when a classical explanation exists.

Current focus: how typical is contextuality?

I'm studying Typicality of Contextuality (Rossi, Zjawin, Baldijão, Schmid, Selby, Sainz), which asks how often randomly chosen states and measurements produce nonclassical statistics. The main findings, as I understand them:

  • A sharp threshold. With n ≤ d² random states (four for a qubit), the scenario is noncontextual with probability one, since linearly independent states cannot witness contextuality. Above this, typicality rises quickly.
  • Pure states and projective measurements. Typicality exceeds 99% at around n = 7 states and m = 8 measurements. It tends to 1 as the number of measurements or states grows, but stays strictly below 1 for any finite scenario.
  • Noise. Mixedness slows the rise but does not remove it. With 20 projective measurements, n = 9 states suffice at purity ≥ 0.7, and n = 7 at purity ≥ 0.9. With unsharp measurements the requirement can reach n = 25.
  • Versus Bell nonlocality. In the prepare-and-measure picture, contextuality reaches near-certain typicality faster than nonlocality. The comparison is not quantitative, because the steered states in a Bell scenario obey no-signalling constraints that random sampling ignores.
  • Typical contextuality is not typical advantage. In 3-to-1 parity-oblivious multiplexing, random projective measurements are contextual 98.6% of the time but give about an 8% advantage over the best noncontextual strategy, against 18.3% for the optimal one. Random POVMs are contextual 15.2% of the time, for a 0.3% advantage.

Questions I'm interested in

  • Typicality of nonclassicality for individual components (states, channels, sets of states) rather than whole experiments, following Zhang, Schmid, Yīng and Spekkens.
  • How these results change in higher dimensions, and whether restricting an experiment to a subspace (for example a qubit inside a qutrit) systematically raises the odds of contextuality.
  • Whether the robustness r predicts task advantage beyond parity-oblivious multiplexing.

Can entanglement through gravity be witnessed in the CMB?

Independent Side Project · Inspired by discussions with Marios Christodoulou (IQOQI Vienna) · [August 2026 - Present]

This is an independent side project alongside my thesis. The question was suggested to me by Marios Christodoulou (IQOQI Vienna), whose work concerns testing the quantum nature of gravity with table-top experiments. At this stage I'm studying the literature and have no results of my own to report.

The question

During inflation, each Fourier mode of the curvature perturbation $\zeta$ is driven by the expanding background into a two-mode squeezed state, in which modes k and −k are entangled in the sense of Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen. Could that entanglement be witnessed in CMB data? And would a positive result say anything about gravity specifically, rather than about the inflaton field?

Where it starts

As Christodoulou pointed out, CMB maps are effectively position-type, equal-time measurements. A witness needs a non-commuting, momentum-like variable as well. Outside the horizon, $\zeta$ is conserved and its conjugate momentum is suppressed by the same squeezing that makes $\zeta$ so robust a cosmological observable. This obstruction is developed in Maldacena's cosmological Bell inequalities and in Martin and Vennin's Obstructions to Bell CMB Experiments. His suggestion that differences of measurements at different redshifts might supply the missing variable connects naturally to Leggett–Garg-type tests, which use one system measured at several times.

What I've been working through

  • Decoherence. The Polarski–Starobinsky picture of squeezing-induced classicality, Hsiang and Hu's argument that squeezing alone cannot produce true decoherence in a closed system, and the open-system calculations (e.g. Burgess et al., 2023) that treat decoherence from short-wavelength modes.
  • Non-Gaussianity. Gaussian squeezed states have non-negative Wigner functions, so any genuine non-classicality must come from non-Gaussian structure. I'm reading Inflation is Not Magic (Haque et al.) and Ireland and Vennin's work on negativity beyond slow roll.
  • Framing. The (k, −k) split is not a privileged bipartition, since a rotation to standing-wave modes makes the same state a product state. The squeezing is driven by a classical background, as in parametric down-conversion with a classical pump. Real-space entanglement is generic to any quantum field. Equal-time statistics of $\zeta$ alone always have a classical stand-in, because the observables commute.
  • The inference to gravity. Anastopoulos and Hu argue that, in the weak-field regime, such experiments probe only gravity's constraint sector, in analogy with the Coulomb term in QED. The claim that entanglement implies a quantum mediator is also under active debate following Aziz and Howl (Nature, 2025).

Open questions I'd like to work on

  • Whether unequal-time observables, such as redshift tomography or Leggett–Garg-type constructions, can supply a usable non-commuting variable for $\zeta$.
  • Whether Ireland and Vennin's Wigner-negativity result for the EFT Goldstone mode transfers to the curvature perturbation at finite wavenumber.
  • Whether the delocalised-source construction of Chen and Giacomini, which goes beyond the Newtonian potential in the laboratory, has a cosmological analogue. As far as I have found, this has not been attempted.

Magnetic fields from ultralight dark matter

Undergraduate Research · Supervised by Prof. Koushik Dutta (IISER Kolkata) · [August 2025 - July 2026]

This was my undergraduate research project at IISER Kolkata, supervised by Prof. Koushik Dutta. It concerned the origin of cosmic magnetic fields, and in particular whether an oscillating ultralight dark matter field can generate them after recombination.

The problem

Blazar observations point to a lower bound of roughly 10−17 G on intergalactic magnetic fields on scales of about 0.1 Mpc and above, inferred from the absence of expected GeV–TeV cascade emission. CMB observations give an upper bound of roughly 10−9 G. The origin of such fields is unknown. Inflationary and phase-transition mechanisms both struggle to deliver a large enough coherence length.

The mechanism

Brandenberger, Fröhlich and Jiao propose that dark matter is an axion-like field ϕ coupled to photons through ϕ F F̃. Once the Hubble rate falls below the field's mass, ϕ oscillates. This makes one circular polarization of the gauge field tachyonically unstable during part of each period, so the resonance produces a helical magnetic field with a spectrum peaked near kc ≃ g m ϕ0 a. The mechanism operates after recombination, when Thomson scattering no longer screens the photon. The resulting field at 1 Mpc is of order 10−15 G, with a steep dependence on the coupling and on a backreaction parameter F, the fraction of the dark matter energy that can drain into photons before the resonance shuts off. The original paper leaves F as a free parameter.

Follow-up papers extend the idea to a scalar (dilaton-like) coupling, which gives no helicity but a time-varying fine-structure constant, to a second narrow-resonance channel with a first backreaction estimate, to seeding supermassive black holes, and to a non-Abelian dark matter condensate acting through an indirect cascade.

The obstruction

Sharma, Majumdar and Sachdeva point out that recombination is never complete. A residual free-electron fraction of about 10−4 gives the post-recombination universe a conductivity σ that exceeds the Hubble rate by roughly 26 orders of magnitude. The gauge-field equation is then heavily overdamped, and the growth rate is suppressed by a factor of order H/σ instead of growing exponentially. A growth rate above the Hubble rate would require a coupling several orders of magnitude above what observations allow. They confirm this numerically with the Pencil Code. Two proposed ways around it are to move the resonance to a dark photon and transfer the field by kinetic mixing (Alexander et al.), and to produce the field earlier, during radiation domination, using a delayed oscillation (Gerlach, Schmitt and Schwaller).

Directions I find interesting

  • Repeating the conductivity analysis for the channels it has not yet been applied to: the scalar/dilaton coupling, the narrow-band resonance, and the non-Abelian cascade.
  • Following the ionization fraction through recombination, to see whether a brief window exists where σ/H is low enough for efficient resonance, instead of using the frozen-out relic value.
  • Forecasting CMB Faraday rotation from post-recombination fields for instruments such as LiteBIRD and CMB-S4.
  • Treating dark matter density fluctuations as noise on the driving term, which should enlarge the Floquet exponent and may soften the conductivity bound.
